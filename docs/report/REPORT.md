# Abstract

Trading education online is mostly video with no model of what the learner actually knows. Everyone gets the same content in the same order, in a domain where the market itself is a poor teacher and most retail traders lose money. This project asked whether a Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) student model can drive a genuinely adaptive learning loop for trading concepts, and whether such a system can produce interaction data good enough to evaluate that adaptation. The answer was built rather than argued. TradeMind Academy is a working web application: a nine-module, 33-lesson curriculum written from four hours of original video, 72 assessment items across six question types, a pure BKT engine that updates a per-concept mastery estimate after every answer, a rule-based router that decides whether to teach, practise or remediate, and an append-only response log recording the model's estimate before and after each response. The learner can inspect the model at any time, including a "why this question?" explanation. Ethics is enforced structurally: simulated data only, an 18+ gate in the database rules, real consent with a working decline, and strict separation between development and research databases. Verification covers 54 unit tests against hand-computed BKT arithmetic, 14 security-rule tests, 24 browser journeys including one that survives a network cut, and a 200-learner simulation confirming the three mastery behaviours committed to at the progress-report stage. The simulation also exposed a real limit: the prior parameter is barely identifiable from 40 responses, which is reported rather than hidden. The planned study with human learners was not completed inside the project window, because the curriculum only reached its final form in September, so learning gain and usability remain the recommended next step. The artefact, its instruments and its dataset design are ready for it.

# 1. Introduction and Background

## 1.1 The problem

Learning to trade has a starting problem, and it's one I've lived. The information isn't hard to find. There's far too much of it, almost none of it is in any order, and a beginner has no reliable way to tell a credible source from a confident stranger. Much of what's free comes from self-styled gurus, and the Financial Conduct Authority has flagged firms using influencers to push high-risk products with unrealistic promises (FCA, 2022). So a learner is left guessing what to study, in what order, and who to trust, and that's where most people give up or pick up habits they later have to unlearn.

The wider evidence base is uncomfortable. Retail traders as a group underperform the market, and the most active ones underperform it most (Barber and Odean, 2000). Among people who trade intraday, fewer than one in a hundred earn reliably positive returns after costs (Barber et al., 2014), and one large study found no evidence that traders improve with experience at all (Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti, 2020). That last finding is the important one for an education project. It says the market itself is a bad teacher. Its feedback is delayed, noisy and confounded, so a sound decision can lose money and a poor one can be paid. Deliberate practice, the thing that actually builds expertise, needs structured tasks and immediate diagnostic feedback (Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, 1993), and live markets supply neither.

Existing trading courses don't fix this. They present identical videos and quizzes in an identical order to every learner, whatever that learner already knows. There's no model of the student, nothing adapts, and the learner never finds out whether they've understood a concept until they've lost money on it. Intelligent tutoring systems were built to address exactly this shape of problem, and they have a mature evidence base showing they come reasonably close to one-to-one human tutoring (VanLehn, 2011).

## 1.2 Research questions

The literature survey in Chapter 2 identified a gap. Knowledge tracing has been applied to mathematics, language and programming, but I could find no published tutoring system that applies a formal student model to trading education. So the questions this project set out to answer are:

- RQ1. Can a trading curriculum be decomposed into knowledge components clean enough for Bayesian Knowledge Tracing to trace, and does the engine behave as the design requires when it does?
- RQ2. Can a BKT-driven routing policy run a complete adaptive learning loop end to end, while recording the data a learning-gain study would need?
- RQ3. Can the learner see and understand the model that's making decisions about them?
- RQ4. What constraints does the regulatory and ethical context impose on an educational trading artefact, and can they be built in without degrading the learning experience?

RQ2 originally included measuring learning gain with human participants. That part wasn't completed, for reasons set out in Chapters 4 and 7, so the question as answered here concerns whether the loop works and whether it produces the right data.

## 1.3 Aim and objectives

The aim was to design, build and evaluate an intelligent tutoring system for trading education whose adaptation is driven by a Bayesian student model. The five objectives were fixed in the AE1 progress report and haven't been changed since, because moving the goalposts after the fact would make the evaluation meaningless:

1. Review the literature on intelligent tutoring, student modelling and trading education to ground the design.
2. Design the architecture and content structure for a mastery-gated trading curriculum.
3. Build a vertical slice of the system end to end, with BKT student modelling and rule-based routing.
4. Validate the adaptive engine technically through unit tests and simulated-learner checks.
5. Evaluate the built slice with real users for learning gain and usability.

Chapter 7 reports on each of these honestly. Four were met. The fifth was not.

## 1.4 Scope

The artefact is a web application, TradeMind Academy, covering one complete curriculum level. A learner signs up, gives informed consent, sits a short placement test, and is then routed by the model through lessons and practice sessions until each module is mastered. Everything is on simulated or historical data. There is no broker connection, no live pricing, no trade signals and no payment code. Three larger features from the original product design (a mentor marketplace, a trading terminal simulator and a generative AI tutor) were cut deliberately and are documented as future work rather than half-built.

## 1.5 Key terms

A knowledge component (KC) is a single teachable skill or concept, small enough to be assessed on its own. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) maintains, for each KC, the probability that a learner has learned it, updated after every observed answer (Corbett and Anderson, 1994). Mastery is a threshold on that probability, set here at 0.8, above which a module counts as learned and the next unlocks. Remediation is the band below 0.4, where the system stops pushing forward and re-teaches. An open learner model shows the learner the system's model of them (Bull and Kay, 2010). The ICT methodology is the practitioner framework the curriculum is drawn from. It's grey literature, and this report makes no claim about whether it makes money; what's studied is whether it can be taught adaptively.

# 2. Literature Survey

## 2.1 Approach

This survey condenses a longer literature review chapter written mid-project. Searches ran between February and July 2026 across the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Google Scholar, SSRN and the Journal of Educational Data Mining archive, with snowballing from three anchor papers (Corbett and Anderson, 1994; VanLehn, 2011; Abdelrahman, Wang and Nunes, 2023). Roughly 240 records were screened. Peer-reviewed empirical work, formal models and systematic syntheses were kept; blog posts and vendor material were excluded, with regulatory publications cited only as evidence of the policy context. Screening was by one researcher and limited to English, both stated limitations. The survey covers five strands: the problem domain, intelligent tutoring, student modelling, content and engagement, and evaluation and ethics. Appendix J traces each major design decision back to its evidence.

## 2.2 Retail trading as a learning problem

The empirical record on retail trading is consistent across markets and decades. Barber and Odean (2000) studied 66,000 US households and found the most active traders earned about six percentage points below the market each year. Barber et al. (2014) analysed every day trader on the Taiwan Stock Exchange over fifteen years and found that skill differences are real and persistent, but fewer than one per cent earned reliably positive returns after fees. Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti (2020) used regulator data from Brazil and found that of individuals who persisted past 300 trading days, 97 per cent lost money, and performance didn't improve with experience.

That absence of learning is what makes this a pedagogy problem and not only a finance one. Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) describe expertise as the product of deliberate practice: structured tasks at the edge of ability, with immediate diagnostic feedback. Live markets offer none of that, and Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) add that minimally guided discovery is inefficient for novices because it leaves them to infer structure from unreliable signals. So the case for this project is narrower than the one the trading education industry makes. It isn't that education produces profitable traders. It's that if any part of trading competence is learnable, unstructured exposure is a poor way to learn it, and decomposed skills with immediate feedback are the standard remedy.

## 2.3 Does financial education work?

The answer has shifted. Lusardi and Mitchell (2014) established that financial literacy is low and correlates with consequential behaviour. But Fernandes, Lynch and Netemeyer (2014) meta-analysed 201 studies and found education explained about 0.1 per cent of the variance in later behaviour, with effects decaying within months. Kaiser et al. (2022) then meta-analysed 76 randomised trials covering over 160,000 people and found positive, economically meaningful effects on knowledge and behaviour that survived correction for publication bias. The two reconcile once you notice the later studies used stronger designs and more interactive delivery: the earlier null was about intervention quality, not education itself.

Two things carry into the design. The decay finding argues for what a tutoring system provides by default: spaced retrieval, remediation when mastery slips, and per-learner sequencing. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) show repeated retrieval produces far better long-term retention than repeated study, which is what a mastery-gated question loop implements. And almost none of this literature is about trading. The trials measure budgeting, saving and credit; peer-reviewed evaluation of instruction in chart-based trading skills is essentially absent, even though a large industry sells it.

## 2.4 Intelligent tutoring systems

The conventional ITS architecture has four parts: a domain model, a student model, a pedagogical model and an interface (Nwana, 1990). The landmark implementations were the Carnegie Mellon cognitive tutors, deployed at classroom scale (Anderson et al., 1995). Bloom (1984) set the field's target with the two sigma problem: one-to-one mastery tutoring moved students about two standard deviations above conventional teaching.

The effectiveness evidence is consistent in direction and varied in size. VanLehn (2011) found intelligent tutors nearly as effective as human tutors (0.76 against 0.79); Ma et al. (2014) found 0.41 against large-group instruction; Kulik and Fletcher (2016) a median 0.66 across 50 evaluations. The counterweight is Pane et al. (2014), a two-year randomised trial of Cognitive Tutor Algebra with around 18,700 students, which found roughly 0.20 in the second year. So tutoring systems reliably beat static instruction, the margin is moderate, and implementation quality dominates. A dissertation claiming large gains from a small evaluation would be contradicted by the strongest study in its own supporting literature.

One further argument shapes the design philosophy. Baker (2016) observes that the systems succeeding at scale are markedly simpler than the research prototypes, drawing their value from solid student modelling and transparent pedagogy rather than elaborate AI. That supports pairing a well-understood probabilistic model with rules a person can read, instead of a learned policy nobody can inspect.

## 2.5 Student modelling and Bayesian Knowledge Tracing

The student model is what makes a tutor intelligent, and knowledge tracing is the standard formulation: given a learner's response history, estimate their current knowledge and predict future performance (Abdelrahman, Wang and Nunes, 2023). The foundational model is BKT (Corbett and Anderson, 1994). Each knowledge component is a two-state hidden Markov model. The learner either knows it or doesn't, and that hidden state is inferred from observed answers through four parameters: the prior probability of knowing, P(L0); the probability of learning at each opportunity, P(T); the probability of guessing correctly while not knowing, P(G); and the probability of slipping while knowing, P(S). After each answer the posterior is updated by Bayes' rule, then the learning transition is applied.

BKT's appeal here is threefold. Its parameters have pedagogical meaning, so the model can be explained to a marker, a learner or an examiner. Its per-KC probability maps directly onto routing decisions and onto a display. And it starts cold gracefully, because the parameter space is small and well characterised, so a system with no data can adopt literature defaults and refit later (Badrinath, Wang and Pardos, 2021; Bulut et al., 2023).

The limitations are well documented. Beck and Chang (2007) showed an identifiability problem: different parameter sets can fit the same responses equally well while implying different knowledge states, though Doroudi and Brunskill (2017) argue its practical severity depends on how the model is used. Model degeneracy, where fitted guess or slip exceed 0.5 and the semantics invert, was characterised by Baker, Corbett and Aleven (2008). Individualised parameters (Yudelson, Koedinger and Gordon, 2013) and per-item difficulty (Pardos and Heffernan, 2011) each improve accuracy. For this project the consequence is procedural: fitted parameters must be checked against bounds and reported, thresholds treated as design decisions, and claims about what the model "knows" hedged.

BKT isn't the only option. Deep Knowledge Tracing (Piech et al., 2015) trains a recurrent network on raw sequences and dominates publication volume, but the accuracy picture is more balanced than that implies. Khajah, Lindsey and Mozer (2016) found BKT with modest extensions largely matches DKT. Gervet et al. (2020), across nine datasets, concluded that deep models lead only on very large corpora, that logistic regression leads on moderate ones, and that calibration matters more than headline accuracy for a system that acts on its predictions. Pelánek (2017) reaches a similar conclusion about choosing by purpose rather than fashion. Table 2.1 makes the trade-off explicit.

Table: Student model selection against this project's constraints

| Model | Interpretable | Cold start | Data needed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BKT | High | Strong, literature defaults | Low | Selected |
| BKT with forgetting | High | Strong | Low to medium | Extension candidate |
| Logistic (PFA) | Medium | Weak | Medium | Rejected, weaker cold start |

A dissertation-scale deployment produces a small dataset, exactly the regime where deep models are inappropriate and interpretability is worth more than a marginal gain in accuracy.

## 2.6 Mastery thresholds and open learner models

Knowledge tracing only matters because of what the tutor does with it. Corbett (2001) showed that mastery learning driven by BKT recovers a meaningful share of Bloom's two sigma advantage inside a computer tutor. The threshold is a design parameter: the original cognitive tutors used 0.95 with large item banks and compulsory classroom use (Corbett and Anderson, 1994), and Pelánek (2018) argues mastery criteria conflate the system's uncertainty with the learner's knowledge and should be treated as consequential decisions. This project uses 0.8 to unlock and 0.4 to remediate, because a voluntary consumer context with smaller item banks punishes over-strict gating with attrition rather than compliance.

A separate strand supports a distinctive interface decision. Bull and Kay (2010) review open learner models, which expose the student model to the student, and report benefits for reflection, self-assessment and trust. TradeMind makes the mastery probabilities visible elements that update after every answer and lets the learner ask why a question was chosen, which is an open learner model in the literature's sense. Doroudi and Brunskill (2019) add an equity argument: mastery-based adaptation narrows but doesn't close the gap between fast and slow learners, which suggests reporting the distribution of gains and not only the mean.

## 2.7 Teaching an ill-structured domain

Nearly every ITS success story comes from a well-structured domain. Algebra, geometry, programming. Trading isn't like that. Lynch et al. (2006) describe what makes domains ill-defined for tutoring: contested correctness, multiple defensible solutions, weak formal theory. Whether a given trade was correct is often unknowable even in hindsight. The response is to decompose the curriculum into components that are individually well-structured even where the whole activity isn't. Reading a candle, classifying a swing sequence, computing a position size: each is objectively checkable. Live trade decision-making is deliberately excluded from what the tutor assesses.

The content needs honest positioning. The curriculum teaches the ICT methodology, a practitioner framework with no peer-reviewed validation. The nearest academic anchor, the technical analysis literature, is mixed: Lo, Mamaysky and Wang (2000) found several chart patterns carry statistically significant information, while Park and Irwin (2007) found profitability evidence concentrated in earlier periods and vulnerable to data snooping. So the framing throughout is that ICT is a structured, teachable body of knowledge whose delivery is the research object. Nothing here claims it's profitable, and that separation is what lets the project be evaluated as computing research rather than as an unregistered financial claim.

## 2.8 Content delivery and engagement

Lessons are short narrated videos paired with the same material as on-page text. Mayer's multimedia principles support that pairing: narration with relevant visuals beats text alone, segmenting into learner-paced units reduces load, and stripped-back presentation beats decoration (Mayer and Moreno, 2003; Mayer, 2021). Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014) found MOOC video engagement dropping sharply beyond about six minutes. Sweller's (1988) cognitive load theory adds a sequencing argument: a populated price chart overwhelms a novice because element interactivity is so high, so the curriculum moves from isolated elements to integrated ones.

On engagement, the system layers mastery rings, unlock ceremonies and badges over the adaptive core. Sailer and Homner (2020) found small to medium positive effects of gamification on learning, and Duolingo shows a game-like surface can sit over a serious statistical learner model (Settles and Meeder, 2016). The constraint is that game mechanics mustn't override pedagogy, so progression here is gated by BKT mastery and the game layer decorates it.

## 2.9 Generative AI tutors

Any tutoring dissertation written in 2026 has to face the obvious challenge: why build a structured tutor when a chatbot can answer any trading question? The strongest evidence for LLM tutoring is Kestin et al. (2025), a randomised trial with 194 physics undergraduates where a purpose-built AI tutor roughly doubled the gains of an already strong active-learning class. It would be dishonest to minimise that. But the tutor wasn't a general chatbot; it was engineered with expert scaffolds and guardrails, in a domain with unambiguous correctness. Letourneau et al. (2025) found evaluation quality across AI tutoring systems uneven, with many assessed on engagement rather than learning. The specific concern for trading is factual reliability: a tutor that hallucinates in a leveraged-trading lesson produces a wrong answer a learner might act on financially. And a conversation doesn't by itself maintain a calibrated, auditable per-skill probability that can gate progression or be exported. So the position taken is complementarity: an inspectable model governs progression and produces the dataset, and generative assistance is designed but deferred.

## 2.10 Evaluating adaptive learning systems

A system like this has to be assessed on three axes. Learning effect is conventionally measured with pre-test and post-test designs using Hake's (1998) normalised gain, which corrects for ceiling effects among learners with higher prior knowledge, with effect sizes judged against realistic education benchmarks (Kraft, 2020) such as the 0.20 in Pane et al. (2014). Model quality is separate from learning gain, because a system can improve learning while its model is poorly calibrated, or the reverse; the convention is prediction accuracy plus calibration, and reporting fitted parameters against degeneracy bounds (Baker, Corbett and Aleven, 2008; Gervet et al., 2020). Usability is assessed with the System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996) against the published average of 68 (Bangor, Kortum and Miller, 2008), with free-text feedback analysed thematically (Braun and Clarke, 2006). A small voluntary cohort is underpowered for confident inference, which is why the planned evaluation was framed as feasibility evidence rather than a hypothesis test.

## 2.11 Regulation and ethics

The regulatory record imposes obligations an algebra tutor wouldn't face. When ESMA intervened in the retail CFD market it cited analyses showing 74 to 89 per cent of retail CFD accounts lose money, and imposed leverage caps, negative balance protection and standardised risk warnings (ESMA, 2018). The FCA made near-identical restrictions permanent in the UK (FCA, 2019) and continues to warn about influencer promotion of high-risk products (FCA, 2022). An academic artefact teaching trading therefore has to be designed so it can't be mistaken for, or drift into, a financial promotion. In TradeMind that's a product feature: education only, simulated data only, 18+, no signals, no live accounts, no broker links, and risk framing in the interface rather than a footer. The interaction log is personal data about learner performance, so collection is minimised, consent is informed, exports are pseudonymised, and the design follows UK GDPR data minimisation and purpose limitation. As a computing project it sits under the BCS Code of Conduct and its public-interest duty (BCS, 2022).

## 2.12 The gap and the traceability of decisions

Read together, these literatures give a coherent picture. Retail trading outcomes are poor and markets teach badly. Well-designed financial education works, but its evidence covers budgeting and saving rather than trading. Intelligent tutors reliably outperform static instruction by a moderate margin, and BKT is mature, interpretable and workable at small scale. What doesn't exist is the intersection. The knowledge tracing literature is built almost entirely on mathematics, language and programming datasets (Abdelrahman, Wang and Nunes, 2023), and this survey found no tutoring system applying a formal student model to trading education. That's where this project sits. Appendix J links each design decision to its evidence so nothing in Chapters 3 to 5 is asserted without a source.

# 4. Methodology

## 4.1 Research approach

The project is design science research. It addresses a problem by building an artefact and evaluating it, in the sense set out by Hevner et al. (2004), and it follows the process Peffers et al. (2007) describe: identify the problem, define objectives for a solution, design and develop, demonstrate, evaluate, communicate. That framing fits because the research question can't be answered by argument. Whether BKT can drive an adaptive loop for trading concepts is something you find out by building the loop and instrumenting it.

The alternative, an empirical study on an existing platform, wasn't available because no existing platform applies a student model to this domain, which is the gap itself. A survey-based needs analysis was considered and rejected as adding little beyond what the regulatory and finance literature already establishes, so the justification for the aims came from the literature rather than from primary survey data.

## 4.2 Development method

Development was iterative and phased, building the whole vertical slice end to end before adding anything around it, so the loop worked early and was then refined. Six phases mirrored the milestone order set in the progress report: foundations and design tokens; authentication, data layer and content layer; assessment engine and question bank; the BKT engine, routing and the visible model; learner-support features; and landing, polish and audits. Each phase had a written done-criterion, and each ended with the verification checklist actually run and its output recorded in the evaluation log before the phase was tagged in version control. The riskiest component, the engine, was reached only once the plumbing around it already worked, which meant every mistake in the engine showed up in a working interface rather than in isolation.

Two practices ran through every phase. Every significant decision was appended to a decisions log with its reason, so the rationale in Chapter 5 comes from a record kept at the time rather than from memory. And "measured, not assumed" was applied wherever it could be: animation costs profiled in frames per second, accessibility audited with Lighthouse rather than eyeballed, the model validated with simulated learners rather than by watching a few numbers go up.

Implementation used Claude Code as an AI pair-programming tool under my direction, as declared in the progress report. The research question, the choice of model and thresholds, the curriculum, the evaluation design and every decision in the log are mine. The distinction matters for academic integrity and it's also why the decisions log exists: it records the reasoning, including cases where something was built, measured and removed.

| Technical correctness | Unit tests on BKT and routing; rules tests; E2E journeys | All tests pass; engine maths matches hand-computed values | Yes |

The first two strands are what a computing dissertation can verify unambiguously, and they're reported in full in Chapter 6. The third and fourth needed human participants. Ethical clearance for that study was obtained through the Solent University ethics application and signed off by my supervisor (Appendix A). The study was not run, and the reason is a matter of record in the decisions log. The curriculum content, 33 lessons written from four hours of original video, only reached its final form between 8 and 10 September 2026. Before that the platform ran on placeholder lessons that existed to exercise the structure. A learning-gain study on placeholder content would have measured the placeholders, not the tutor, and usability feedback on lessons about to be replaced would have been feedback on the wrong thing. So the choice was between a study whose results would be meaningless and completing the content the study needed, and I chose the content. Chapter 7 evaluates that decision and Chapter 8 sets out the study as the immediate next step.

## 4.4 Technical testing method

Testing followed a pyramid. At the base, Vitest unit tests cover the BKT update mathematics against fixtures I computed by hand from the equations (Appendix C), the threshold crossings, the routing decision table, the difficulty ladder, non-repetition within a session, the remediation trigger, grading per question type, and the logger's queue, retry, persistence and backoff. Hand-computed fixtures test the maths rather than the code's opinion of itself; if the implementation drifts, it fails against arithmetic. The security rules have their own suite inside the Firestore emulator that attempts every forbidden operation and asserts refusal. At the top, Playwright drives a real browser through the journeys on desktop and Pixel 7 profiles: sign-up through consent to a lesson; a ten-question mixed-type session with the network cut halfway; placement through routing to mastery and an unlock; the learner-support screens; and keyboard and reduced-motion accessibility. A pre-commit hook and CI run lint, typecheck and unit tests on every commit.

## 4.5 Simulated-learner method

The simulation harness validates the engine against learners whose true state is known, which no human study can do. It generates 200 synthetic learners with ground-truth parameters drawn from ranges (prior uniform on 0.05 to 0.45, learn rate uniform on 0.05 to 0.25, guess and slip fixed at 0.2 and 0.1), has each answer 40 opportunities through the true generative model, and runs the real engine over those responses. It then checks three behaviours committed to at AE1: that a streak of correct answers crosses 0.8 within a few items, that a streak of wrong answers stays below 0.4, and that learners whose true state improves are tracked upward. It also recovers each learner's prior and learn rate by maximum-likelihood grid search over the response sequence, and reports root mean squared error and bias against ground truth. The run is seeded, so it reproduces to the digit, and its output is written straight into the evaluation log. This mirrors the sanity-check approach the pyBKT authors use (Badrinath, Wang and Pardos, 2021).

## 4.6 Planned human study design

Because it's the recommended next step, the approved design is as follows. Five to eight adult participants recruited by convenience sampling. Each completes the placement test, which doubles as a pre-test since its items come from the pre-test-eligible subset of the bank, works through the first module or two in one session, and sits a post-test from the same subset. Learning gain is Hake's normalised gain per participant, reported with the spread and not only the mean, following Doroudi and Brunskill's (2019) equity argument. Usability is the ten-item SUS compared with the 68 average, plus a short open questionnaire analysed thematically. Every logged response carries the model's estimate before the answer, and the predicted probability of a correct answer is a fixed function of that estimate and the parameters, so calibration can be computed from the log. The sample is a known limitation, and the study was framed as feasibility evidence, not a powered trial.

## 4.7 Professional, Legal and Ethical issues

So the constraints were built as features rather than disclaimers. Every chart is simulated or historical and says so in a pill on the chart itself. There's no live market data, no broker connection, no affiliate links, no trade signals and no profitability language anywhere in the copy, and Chapter 6 reports a sweep for exactly those things. The landing page carries a full risk disclaimer, the sign-up form states the education-only purpose above the fields, and the curriculum is honest that the methodology it teaches is contested. The mentor marketplace was cut specifically because paid guidance from strangers is the mechanism the FCA warns about.

Under UK GDPR the lawful basis for collecting learning data is consent. The 18+ requirement is enforced in the database security rules and not only in the interface, so a profile can't be created without the attestation whichever route a learner takes, including Google sign-in. Data is minimised to what the model and evaluation need. Learners can download all of their data as CSV and delete their account from settings. The response log is append-only by rule, which protects the research dataset from tampering and also protects learners from accidental loss; deletion of that log on request is done by an administrator so it can't happen silently. Development and testing run on a local emulator that's wiped on restart, and the live project is reserved for participants, with the split enforced by configuration after an early incident described in Section 5.10.

# 5. Design and Implementation

## 5.1 Architecture

The architecture follows the six-component design fixed at AE1 and the ITS structure from the literature. A content layer holds lessons and items. An assessment engine serves and grades questions. A student model estimates mastery per knowledge component. A control layer of routing rules reads that estimate and decides what happens next. A progress interface shows the learner the model and its decisions. And an authentication and data layer holds accounts and the research log. Figure 5.1 shows the components and the single loop they form.

![System architecture: six components and the adaptive loop](docs/report-figures/fig-architecture.png)

Two boundaries were treated as inviolable. The BKT engine and the routing rules are pure TypeScript modules with no framework or database imports, so they're trivially unit-testable and portable. And the response logger is the only path by which an answer reaches the database, so there's exactly one place where the research log can be got right or wrong.

## 5.2 Data model and security rules

Firestore holds a user document per learner with consent state, the 18+ flag and settings; one mastery document per course containing the per-KC estimates, attempts and timestamps; a session document per placement, practice or review session; and beneath each session, one response document per answer. That last collection is the research dataset. Each response carries the item, its KC, the question type, whether it was correct, what was selected, the latency in milliseconds, and the model's estimate before and after the update. Content lives in four collections: courses, KCs (with prerequisite lists), lessons and items (with answer keys and explanations).

The security rules (Appendix D) deny everything by default. Learners can read and write only their own subtree. A user document can only be created with the adult flag set to true, which is the 18+ gate. Sessions can't be deleted. Responses can be created and read by their owner and can never be updated or deleted by anyone through the client, including the owner. Content is readable by any signed-in user and writable only with an administrator claim, which the seed script holds and the client never does. Fourteen rules tests cover both the permitted operations and every forbidden one.

## 5.3 The BKT engine

The engine implements classic BKT (Corbett and Anderson, 1994) as a pure function. Given a current estimate and an observed answer, it conditions on the evidence and then applies the learning transition:

- After a correct answer: P(L | correct) = P(L)(1 − P(S)) / [P(L)(1 − P(S)) + (1 − P(L))P(G)]
- After a wrong answer: P(L | wrong) = P(L)P(S) / [P(L)P(S) + (1 − P(L))(1 − P(G))]
- Learning step: P(L′) = P(L | evidence) + (1 − P(L | evidence))P(T)
- Prediction: P(correct) = P(L)(1 − P(S)) + (1 − P(L))P(G)

Default parameters are the literature-standard 0.25, 0.12, 0.20 and 0.10 for prior, learn, guess and slip, held in one constants object and configurable per KC. The mastery threshold of 0.8 and the remediation threshold of 0.4 are exported constants, so the same numbers the report cites are the numbers the code uses, and nothing else in the codebase is allowed to restate them. The engine also exposes a placement initialiser that runs the update sequence over the placement answers from the default prior, so a learner's starting map reflects what they already know rather than a flat 0.25 everywhere. The whole file is about a hundred lines, of which fifteen are the mathematics.

Placement responses are logged with the estimate before and after set equal, because no learning step is applied during measurement. That keeps placement evidence separately analysable in the dataset.

## 5.4 Routing

Routing is the pedagogical model, and it's deliberately a set of rules a person can read rather than a learned policy. Figure 5.2 shows the decision flow. A KC is unlocked when every prerequisite is at or above 0.8. The target KC is the lowest-mastery unlocked one that isn't yet mastered. If it has never been practised, the action is to teach: the learner is sent to the lesson first. Otherwise the mastery band decides: below 0.4 remediate, between 0.4 and 0.8 practise, at or above 0.8 advance. Item selection follows a difficulty ladder (easy below 0.4, medium below 0.7, hard above), forces an easy item after two consecutive wrongs on a KC, and never repeats an item within a session. When a KC crosses 0.8 the router reports which KCs have newly unlocked so the interface can run the ceremony.

![Routing rules: from mastery map to next action and item](docs/report-figures/fig-routing.png)

The router returns the reason for its choice alongside the item: the current estimate, its band, the difficulty chosen and the predicted probability of a correct answer. That object is what the "why this question?" popover displays, with no translation layer, so the explanation can't drift out of sync with the decision.

## 5.5 The response logger

The logger was designed to behave like a payments system: it must never silently fail. Every submit enqueues an event, persists the queue to local storage, returns immediately so the interface stays responsive, and flushes in the background with exponential backoff from one second to a cap of thirty. A failed write surfaces a toast and re-queues the event. Unflushed events survive a refresh and flush on the next start or on the browser's online event.

One detail deserves explanation because it's the kind of bug that never shows in a demo. The Firebase SDK has its own internal write queue. If the transport was allowed to accept a write while the browser was offline, both queues would hold the same event and both would deliver it on reconnect, producing duplicate rows in the research log months later. So the transport fails fast when the browser reports itself offline, which makes my queue the single retry authority. The E2E suite verifies this by cutting the network halfway through a ten-question session and then counting the responses that reach the emulator's Firestore against the answers actually given.

## 5.7 The assessment engine

Each question type has its own renderer and grader. Numeric items accept a tolerance, ordering grades the sequence, the annotation type renders a real chart and grades a tapped marker against a correct zone that's then revealed, and true/false records a confidence value alongside the answer. The quiz runs in a focus-mode shell with a segmented progress bar, keyboard operation (number keys select, Enter submits), and a session store persisted to local storage so a mid-session refresh resumes at the same question. Feedback is deliberate about tone: correct answers animate with a spring, wrong answers are shown in amber, slower and softer, and never flash red or shake, because a wrong answer in a learning system shouldn't feel like punishment. The quiz background is static, because anything drifting beside a question would confound the response latency the log records.

## 5.9 Testing

The test suites are described in Section 4.4 and their results in Chapter 6. Two engineering choices are worth recording. ## 6.3 BKT engine behaviour

Figure 6.1 plots the engine's output for three ten-answer patterns, computed by calling the real engine with the default parameters. A learner who answers correctly every time moves from 0.25 to 0.648 after one answer and 0.905 after two, crossing the 0.8 unlock line on the second correct answer. A learner who is wrong every time falls to 0.155 and settles at a floor of 0.137, never leaving the remediation band. A learner who is wrong four times and then right six times sits on the same floor, then jumps to 0.487 and 0.833 on the next two correct answers, crossing the line on the sixth response. These are the same three trajectories sketched at AE1, now produced by the shipped code rather than a prototype, and the numbers match the hand-computed unit-test fixtures (0.25 to 0.648 to 0.905).

![BKT engine output for three answer patterns, thresholds shown](docs/report-figures/fig-bkt-trajectories.png)

The speed of the climb is worth a comment. With guess at 0.2 and slip at 0.1, two correct answers are strong evidence, so mastery can be reached in two or three items once a learner is competent. That's a consequence of the literature defaults, and one of the things fitted parameters would change (Section 6.4).

## 6.4 Simulated-learner validation

Table 6.2 reports the harness output for 200 learners over 40 opportunities with seed 42. All three behaviours committed to at AE1 hold. A correct streak crosses 0.8 in two items. A wrong streak never rises above 0.155. And 99 per cent of the cohort scores higher in the last quarter of their sequence than the first, which is what the generative model should produce and what the engine should track.

Table: Simulated-learner harness output (seed 42, reproducible)

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Learners | 200 |
| Opportunities per learner | 40 |
| Sanity 1: correct streak crosses 0.8 | 2 items, pass |
| Sanity 2: wrong streak stays below 0.4 | maximum 0.1552, pass |
| Sanity 3: improving learners climb | 99.0% of cohort, pass |
| RMSE of recovered prior P(L0) | 0.2348 (bias −0.1023) |

| Ground truth ranges | P(L0) uniform 0.05 to 0.45; P(T) uniform 0.05 to 0.25; P(G) 0.2, P(S) 0.1 fixed |

The parameter recovery result is the honest part of this table, and Figure 6.2 shows why a single RMSE understates it. The learn rate recovers reasonably: fitted values track the truth with a positive bias and a cluster at the top of the search grid. The prior barely recovers at all. Its fitted values collapse to the two edges of the grid, 0.025 or 0.475, almost regardless of the true value, giving the bimodal band in the left panel. That's the identifiability problem Beck and Chang (2007) described, appearing exactly where the theory predicts: once a few opportunities have passed and the learn rate has lifted the estimate, the response sequence carries very little information about where it started, and 40 binary observations aren't enough to pin it down. The practical implication is that per-KC priors shouldn't be fitted from short sequences; they should come from the placement test, which is what the engine already does, and the learn rate is the parameter worth fitting from logged data.

## 6.5 Audits

Lighthouse accessibility scored 95 to 100 on every page at the July audit against a gate of 95, the landing and legal pages held back by a colour-contrast failure; after the September token fix both score 100. Dashboard performance is 86 against a gate of 85, up from 68 after the changes in Table 5.2; the landing page scores 88. Keyboard and reduced-motion behaviour are asserted by tests rather than audited by hand: the quiz is fully keyboard operable with correct ARIA states and live announcements, skill map nodes are focusable and open their panel with Enter, and under reduced motion the landing and dashboard render completely with the focus ring visible on the primary action.

The animation work was measured rather than assumed. A rotating blurred layer cost 120 to 19 frames per second on the dashboard and was removed. Each animated full-screen layer cost about 18 frames of compositor budget, while identical layers held static cost nothing measurable, so the final design keeps every visual layer and animates one, at 105 frames per second against a 120 baseline. Visual richness is close to free; continuous animation is expensive.

## 6.6 Guardrail sweep

A final sweep of every string in the interface found no live data, no broker links, no signal language and no profitability claims, and every chart surface carries a simulated-data pill. Decline is covered by an E2E test confirming the learner is signed out and bounced from the learning area. Download-my-data produces a CSV with the same rows the research export uses, and Delete-account is gated by typing DELETE with a five-second undo.

## 7.2 Against the research questions

RQ1 asked whether a trading curriculum decomposes cleanly enough for BKT and whether the engine behaves as required. The decomposition worked: nine components, each objectively assessable, in a prerequisite chain that had to be re-sequenced once because the recording order violated it. The engine behaves exactly as the design requires on every committed check. What the simulation added is a limit the design hadn't anticipated in that form: the prior is not recoverable from short sequences, so the placement test is doing more of the modelling work than the specification originally assumed, and that's a finding rather than a failure.

RQ2 as narrowed asked whether a BKT-driven policy can run the complete loop while producing the right data. It can. The loop runs end to end under test, survives a network cut, and every response carries the fields a learning-gain and calibration analysis needs: the estimate before and after, correctness and latency. The learning-gain half remains open.

RQ3 asked whether the learner can see and understand the model. The system exposes its estimates after every answer, explains each routing decision with the actual decision object, and shows the whole curriculum as a route lit to mastery. Whether learners find that understandable is a usability question that needs participants, so RQ3 is answered on the design side only.

RQ4 asked what constraints the regulatory context imposes and whether they can be met without degrading the experience. The constraints are set out in Section 4.7 and every one is enforced structurally. Nothing in the test results or audits suggests the experience suffered for it; the accessibility and performance gates were met with the constraints in place.

## 7.3 Evaluation of the product

The artefact goes beyond what a taught module delivers in several respects. The engine is pure and hand-verified rather than imported. The research log is designed with the care of a financial ledger, and its hardest failure mode was found and closed before any participant could hit it. Security is by rule and tested. Accessibility met its gate on every page, as a gate rather than a finish, and scores full marks on the pages re-audited in September. And the design decisions were recorded as they were made, including the ones reversed, which is the part of a codebase that usually goes missing.

The weaknesses are real too. The parameters are literature defaults, not fitted, and the simulation shows the climb to mastery is fast under those defaults; a fitted learn rate would likely slow it and make the gating more conservative. The item bank has eight items per module, which is thin for repeated practice and for a pre-test and post-test drawn from the same pool. One question type, chart annotation, has only a single item. And the curriculum, for all the work in it, teaches a contested methodology, which the interface says clearly but which limits how far any learning-gain result would generalise beyond it.

The supporting products hold up. The specification, decisions log, evaluation log, parked-features record and running guide together let a reader reconstruct why the system is the way it is, and the presentation script written for the demonstration draws on the same sources.

## 7.4 Evaluation of the process

The phased method worked as intended: every phase ended with its checks actually run, and the engine was integrated into a working interface from the start. Keeping the decisions log was the single most valuable practice, because the implementation chapter is reconstructed from it rather than remembered. "Measured, not assumed" earned its keep when it removed an effect that looked good and cost eighty per cent of the frame budget.

The process failed in one way and was lucky in another. It failed on sequencing, as above. And it was lucky that the leak of test users into the live database happened before any real participant existed; the environment split should have been there from the day the live project was created. AI-assisted development compressed the build far below the planned ten weeks, and that changed the shape of the project more than anything else. It moved the constraint from writing code to writing content and running evaluation, and a plan written with that in mind would have looked very different.

# 8. Recommendations for Further Work

The recommendations are in priority order, and the first is the one that matters.

Run the human study. Everything it needs exists: ethics approval, a consent flow, a placement test that doubles as a pre-test, 21 pre-test-eligible items, a response log carrying the model's estimate before and after every answer, a CSV export, and the SUS. Five to eight participants over one session each would produce feasibility evidence for learning gain, calibration and usability, and the analysis plan in Section 4.6 is ready. This should happen before any further feature work, because it's the only thing that turns the artefact into an answered research question.

Fit the learn rate from real data and leave the prior to placement. The simulation showed which parameter is identifiable from short sequences and which isn't. Once logged data exists, fitting per-KC learn rates with pyBKT (Badrinath, Wang and Pardos, 2021), checking them against degeneracy bounds (Baker, Corbett and Aleven, 2008), and reporting them would replace the defaults with something defensible, and would probably slow the climb to mastery to a more conservative pace.

Deepen the item bank before scaling participants. Eight items per module is enough to demonstrate the loop and thin for repeated practice. Sixteen per module, with more annotation items, would let the difficulty ladder and the review queue work as designed and would separate pre-test and post-test pools properly.

Add forgetting and spacing. Standard BKT assumes no forgetting. The review queue already flags fading skills after three days by heuristic; a BKT variant with a forgetting transition (Khajah, Lindsey and Mozer, 2016) would put that on a modelled footing and give the spaced-retrieval mechanism from Section 2.3 a principled schedule.

Complete the curriculum's gaps. The most valuable missing topic is backtesting, because it's the thing that would let a learner turn the methodology into their own evidence. Trade journaling, trading psychology and a market-structure vocabulary lesson are the next candidates.

Add a constrained generative tutor. The design deferred this for the reasons in Section 2.9. The right shape is now clearer: an assistant that can only speak to the KC currently being studied, grounded in that lesson's text, with the BKT model unchanged as the sole authority over progression. That keeps the auditable model and adds free-text help where it's useful.

Deploy for remote participants. The live project is configured and Google sign-in is enabled; hosting is a short job. The parked designs, a mentor marketplace and a terminal simulator, should stay parked until the research question is answered, and the marketplace should probably stay parked for good, for the regulatory reasons that cut it.

## Appendix C: BKT worked example and unit-test fixtures

The unit tests assert the engine against values computed by hand from the update equations with the default parameters (P(L0) 0.25, P(T) 0.12, P(G) 0.20, P(S) 0.10). The first two steps of a correct streak:

- Prior 0.25. Correct answer. Evidence: 0.25 × 0.9 / (0.25 × 0.9 + 0.75 × 0.2) = 0.225 / 0.375 = 0.6. Learning step: 0.6 + 0.4 × 0.12 = 0.648.
- Prior 0.648. Correct answer. Evidence: 0.648 × 0.9 / (0.648 × 0.9 + 0.352 × 0.2) = 0.5832 / 0.6536 = 0.8923. Learning step: 0.8923 + 0.1077 × 0.12 = 0.9052.

So the estimate crosses the 0.8 threshold on the second correct answer, which is the "Sanity 1: 2 items" result in Table 6.2. The first step of a wrong streak: evidence 0.25 × 0.1 / (0.25 × 0.1 + 0.75 × 0.8) = 0.025 / 0.625 = 0.04; learning step 0.04 + 0.96 × 0.12 = 0.1552, which is the "maximum 0.1552" in the same table. ## Appendix I: Planned evaluation instruments

The System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996), ten statements each rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), scored by the standard method to give a value out of 100 and compared with the published average of 68:

1. I think that I would like to use this system frequently.
2. I found the system unnecessarily complex.
3. I thought the system was easy to use.
4. I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system.
5. I found the various functions in this system were well integrated.
6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in this system.
7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly.
8. I found the system very cumbersome to use.
9. I felt very confident using the system.
10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.

Open questions to follow: What did you understand the mastery numbers to mean? Did the "why this question?" explanation make sense to you? Was there a moment where the system's choice of what to do next felt wrong? Is there anything you would change?

Learning gain: the placement test (one pre-test-eligible item per module, nine items) serves as the pre-test; the post-test draws a different pre-test-eligible item per module from the remaining twelve. Eight modules have at least two eligible items; Liquidity and Wicks currently has one, so a second would need authoring before the study (Chapter 8). Normalised gain per participant is (post − pre) / (maximum − pre) following Hake (1998), reported individually and as a distribution. Calibration is computed from the response log: the predicted probability of a correct answer follows from the logged pre-answer estimate as P(L)(1 − P(S)) + (1 − P(L))P(G), and is compared with the observed outcome, binned by predicted probability.
