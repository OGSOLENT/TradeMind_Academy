## The fixed-risk principle

The core idea, and it inverts how most beginners think:

> **The risk amount stays the same. The position size changes to keep it the same.**

Three trades, three different stop distances, one risk amount:

| Trade | Stop loss | Risk amount |
|---|---|---|
| A | 10 pts | **$500** |
| B | 25 pts | **$500** |
| C | 40 pts | **$500** |

The stop is placed where the *setup* says it belongs — at invalidation, the protected swing (Lesson 9). It is never moved to suit a position size. Instead the **quantity** is calculated so that, if the stop is hit, the loss equals the same fixed amount every time.

A wide stop therefore means a *smaller* position, not more risk. A tight stop means a larger position for the same money at risk.

### Why fixed

Because it makes every trade comparable. If one loss costs $500 and another costs $2,000, your results are not a measure of your edge — they are a measure of which trades happened to be sized large. Consistency in sizing is what lets the win rate and the R multiple mean anything at all.

The journal image is the whole lesson in one page: a column of identical `$500` entries, trade after trade.

## An 80% win rate that loses money

The video's central demonstration:

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Win rate | **80%** |
| Risk on winners | $50 |
| Risk on the loser | $2,000 |
| **Net P&L** | **Negative** |

Eight wins out of ten, and the account still goes backwards, because the single loss was sized forty times larger than the wins. Being right most of the time is worthless if the times you are wrong cost more than all the times you were right.

> Win rate without consistent sizing tells you nothing.

This is why position sizing sits before the entry patterns in this course: an excellent setup, sized inconsistently, is still a losing system.

## Losses and gains are not symmetrical

Shown as two charts side by side:

| Direction | Label |
|---|---|
| Negative trend | **CRITICAL** |
| Positive growth | **MINOR** |

A 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover. Damage compounds faster than repair, which is why the fixed-risk rule protects the downside first and lets the upside look after itself.

## What happens without it

The failure modes shown:

- **Margin call** — "account requires immediate funding, limits strictly breached"
- **Trailing drawdown** on a funded-trader dashboard, with the account evaluation warning at the threshold

Both are the same event: risk that was not fixed in advance, met by a limit that was.

A **trailing drawdown** is a floor that rises as your account grows — so a run of oversized losses can breach it even from a profitable peak. Sizing consistently is what keeps you away from that floor.

## Key terms

- **Fixed risk** — the same monetary amount at risk on every trade
- **Position size** — the variable that changes so risk stays fixed
- **R multiple** — a result expressed as a multiple of the amount risked
- **Drawdown** — the fall from a peak in account value
- **Trailing drawdown** — a drawdown limit that follows the account's high-water mark

## Common mistakes

- **Moving the stop to fit the position size.** Backwards: the stop is set by structure, the size is calculated from it.
- **Judging yourself on win rate.** Meaningless unless every trade risked the same.
- **Sizing up after losses to "get it back".** The asymmetry above makes this the fastest route to a breached limit.
