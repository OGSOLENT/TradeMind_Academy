## How do you know when to take profits?

Entries have had five lessons. Exits have had passing mentions. This lesson makes the target as mechanical as the entry.

## The manipulation leg

> **Manipulation leg — the specific price swing from a low to the preceding high, measured for targets.**

It is the move that *created your setup*: the sweep that trapped participants, from its extreme back to the level it came from. You measure that leg and project multiples of it forward.

The logic: the size of the manipulation reflects the energy behind the move. A large manipulation leg implies a large expansion; a small one implies a modest target. The projection scales the target to the setup rather than guessing at a fixed distance.

### Change in State of Delivery, again

> **Change in State of Delivery — the critical moment in Candle 2 where price reverses, signalling a direction shift.**

The CISD tells you the leg is complete and ready to measure. Before it, the leg might still be extending.

## The projection ladder

Anchor at the leg, then read the levels:

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| **1 & 0** | The anchors — the leg itself |
| **-1** | Target 1 |
| **-2 to -2.5** | **Primary target** |
| **-4 to -4.5** | Maximum expansion |

**-1** is one full leg-length beyond the anchor: conservative, frequently reached, a sensible place to take partial profit.

**-2 to -2.5** is the primary objective — where a normal successful expansion tends to complete.

**-4 to -4.5** is reserved for maximum-momentum conditions. The video is explicit that the **-4** extension is used when the manipulation leg is large:

> **When we have a large manipulation leg I can use that negative one standard deviation.**

Big manipulation, big expansion. The leg size tells you which rung of the ladder is realistic.

## Setting up the tool

Three steps, in order:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1. Wait** | Wait for the Candle 2 closure and the delivery change |
| **2. Identify** | Find the manipulation leg — low to high |
| **3. Anchor** | Drag the Fibonacci tool from low to high |

The Fibonacci tool is used here purely as a **measuring device** — the retracement ratios are irrelevant. What matters are the negative extensions: -1, -2, -2.5, -4.

Step 1 exists because anchoring before the closure means anchoring to a leg that has not finished forming, which moves every target.

## Confluence — why projections work

Here is the part that turns a mathematical exercise into a tradable target:

> **These projections also align with liquidity targets... equal highs sitting here.**

A projection landing in empty space is a number. A projection landing on **equal highs**, an old swing high, or a previous week's extreme is a number *and* a liquidity pool — two independent reasons for price to travel there.

| Target type | Confluence |
|---|---|
| Old highs / lows | Swept liquidity |
| -2 to -2.5 SD | Primary mathematical projection |
| -4 SD | Extended projection |

When the maths and the map agree, the target is strong. When they disagree, prefer the liquidity — price is drawn to orders, not to numbers.

## Power of confluence

The final section stacks everything: market structure, projections, liquidity. The more independent reasons pointing at one price, the more confident the target. This is the same principle as the formation checklist in Lesson 10, applied to exits instead of entries.

## Key terms

- **Manipulation leg** — the swing from the low to the preceding high, measured for targets
- **Anchor (1 & 0)** — the two ends of the measured leg
- **-2 to -2.5 SD** — the primary projected target zone
- **-4 SD** — maximum expansion, for large manipulation legs
- **Confluence** — independent reasons agreeing on the same price

## Common mistakes

- **Anchoring before the delivery change.** The leg may not be complete.
- **Using Fibonacci retracement levels.** This method uses the negative *extensions*.
- **Taking -4 as standard.** It is the maximum-expansion case, not the default.
