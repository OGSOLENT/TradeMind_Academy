## The question this lesson answers

Lesson 20 taught SMT divergence: two correlated markets disagreeing at a high or low. This lesson tells you which markets to pair, and gives you a second use for the pairing: confirmation of the bias itself.

## The dollar is the hub

> **The Dollar Index (DXY) measures the dollar against a basket of major currencies. Every dollar pair is, in part, a bet on it.**

So the correlations fall out directly:

| Market | Relationship to DXY | Why |
|---|---|---|
| EURUSD | Inverse. Dollar up, euro down. | The euro is most of the basket. |
| GBPUSD | Inverse | Same side of the quote as the euro |
| AUDUSD, NZDUSD | Inverse, and more risk-sensitive | Commodity and risk currencies |
| USDJPY, USDCHF, USDCAD | Direct. Dollar up, pair up. | The dollar is the base |
| Gold | Usually inverse | Priced in dollars |

The first use is simple: if your daily bias on EURUSD is bullish, the dollar index should be showing a bearish bias, or at least not a bullish one. When they agree, the bias is stronger. When the dollar looks bullish and you want to buy the euro, one of your reads is wrong.

## Indices move together, mostly

The three US index futures, the S&P (ES), the Nasdaq (NQ) and the Dow (YM), move together most of the time. They're the standard SMT pairing. If NQ makes a new low and ES doesn't, that's the divergence from Lesson 20, and it's one of the cleanest tells that a sweep is manipulation rather than a real break.

They also tend to move against the dollar and against bond yields, though those relationships loosen and tighten over months. Check them, don't assume them.

## Risk on, risk off

A useful frame for indices and the risk currencies:

> **Risk on: indices up, AUD and NZD up, dollar and yen down. Risk off: the reverse.**

It's a tendency, not a law, but it explains a lot of days. On a risk-off day, buying the Nasdaq and buying AUDUSD are the same bet, and if the dollar is expanding higher, both are fighting it.

## Two uses, side by side

**Confirming the bias.** Before the session, check the correlated market's daily. Do they agree? A bullish index bias with a bearish dollar and a bullish AUD is a coherent picture. A bullish index bias with a strongly bullish dollar is a picture with a hole in it.

**Confirming the sweep.** Inside the kill zone, when your market runs a low, check whether its pair ran the equivalent low. If yours swept and the pair didn't, you've got SMT, and the sweep is much more likely to be the manipulation you were waiting for. If both swept cleanly and both keep going, treat it as expansion, not a sweep.

## What correlation is not

It isn't a signal on its own. "The dollar is up, so sell the euro" is not a trade; it's context. The trade still needs the sweep, the shift and the gap. Correlation is the layer above, in Lesson 43's terms: it belongs in bias and narrative, and it makes the confirmation more believable.

Correlations also drift. The euro and the dollar are locked together by construction. Indices and the dollar are not, and there are months where they move together. Glance at a few weeks of both charts side by side before you lean on a relationship.

## Key terms

- **DXY**: the Dollar Index, the reference for every dollar pair
- **Inverse / direct**: moving opposite to, or with, the dollar
- **Risk on / risk off**: indices and risk currencies up or down together
- **SMT pairing**: two correlated markets read side by side at a sweep

## Common mistakes

- **Trading correlation as a signal.** It's context for the bias, not an entry.
- **Assuming the index-dollar relationship.** It drifts. Check it.
- **Forgetting the base currency.** USDJPY moves with the dollar, not against it.
- **Pairing unrelated markets for SMT.** ES with NQ, EURUSD with GBPUSD or DXY. Not gold with the Nasdaq.
