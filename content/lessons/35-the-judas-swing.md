## The question this lesson answers

Last lesson named the manipulation phase. This one puts a clock on it. The Judas swing is the manipulation happening at a session open, and once you know the time it's due, it stops catching you out.

## The betrayal at the open

> **Judas swing.** A false move at a session open, against the day's true direction, that runs liquidity and then reverses.

The name is the whole idea: it looks like the day's direction, it gets people to commit, and then it turns on them. Traders who chase the open get stopped when price reverses. Traders who understand it wait for it and enter after the reversal.

On a bullish day, the Judas swing is a drop at the open that takes out the lows sitting beneath it. On a bearish day, it's a rally that takes out the highs.

## Where it happens

There are two opens that matter most, and the swing tends to use the first hour of each:

| Open | New York time | What gets swept |
|---|---|---|
| London | Roughly 2am to 5am | The Asian session's high or low |
| New York | Roughly 7am to 10am, especially after 9:30 | The London session's high or low, or the previous day's |

The London Judas swing is the classic one. Asia builds a tight range overnight, London opens, price spikes out of one side of that range to run the stops resting there, and then the real move starts in the opposite direction. If your daily bias is bullish, the spike you want to see is *down*, through the Asian low.

The New York Judas swing is the same trick at the 9:30 equity open. There's a version keyed to the earlier 7am to 8:30am window as well, but the mechanics are identical.

## Telling it from a breakout

Here's the honest problem: in the moment, a Judas swing and a real breakout look the same for the first few candles. Both trade through the level. The differences show up quickly, though:

| Judas swing | Real breakout |
|---|---|
| Against the higher-timeframe bias | With it |
| Runs a clean pool of stops, then stalls | Displaces and keeps going |
| Reverses with a structure shift within the hour | Holds the level on the retest |
| Leaves a wick, not a body, beyond the level | Closes bodies beyond it |

The first row does most of the work. If your daily bias is bullish and price breaks *down* at the London open, you already suspect Judas. If it breaks up, you suspect a real expansion, and you look to join it on the retracement.

## The entry

1. Set the bias from Module 8. Say bullish.
2. Mark the Asian range high and low, and the previous day's low.
3. At the London open, watch for the drop through one of those lows. That's the sweep.
4. Wait for the Market Structure Shift back up, with displacement and a gap.
5. Enter on the retracement into the gap, below the open. Stop below the Judas low.
6. Target the liquidity above: the Asian high, then the previous day's high.

Everything in that list is a lesson you've already done. The Judas swing is just the schedule that tells you when to expect step 3.

## When it doesn't come

Some days there's no Judas swing. Price opens and just goes. That's a strong-trend day, and it usually means the manipulation happened earlier, often in the Asian session itself. Don't invent a sweep that isn't there. If there's no false move by the end of the window, the setup for that session has passed, and the New York open is the next chance.

## Key terms

- **Judas swing**: the false move at a session open that runs stops before reversing
- **Asian range**: the overnight range whose high and low are the London Judas targets
- **The window**: the first hour or so of the London and New York opens

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing the open.** The first move of the session is the one most likely to reverse.
- **Fading a breakout with the bias.** Judas is against the bias. A break with it is expansion.
- **Entering on the sweep alone.** Wait for the shift back.
- **Forcing it.** Some sessions don't have one.
