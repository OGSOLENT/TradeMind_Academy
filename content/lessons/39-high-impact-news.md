## The question this lesson answers

Some days the plan from Lesson 36 doesn't work, and it's not because you read the chart wrong. It's because the calendar was in charge. This lesson is how to read the calendar so those days don't surprise you.

## The releases that matter

Most economic data is noise for a day trader. A few releases aren't:

| Release | When (New York) | What it moves |
|---|---|---|
| **NFP** (Non-Farm Payrolls) | First Friday of the month, 8:30am | Everything, especially the dollar and indices |
| **CPI** (Consumer Price Index) | Monthly, 8:30am | Everything. The inflation print. |
| **FOMC** (the Fed decision) | Eight times a year, 2pm, with a press conference at 2:30pm | Everything, for the rest of the week |
| **PPI**, **GDP**, **retail sales** | Various, mostly 8:30am | Usually a smaller version of the above |

Filter any economic calendar to high-impact only, and these are what's left. Mark them on your chart a week ahead. Note the 8:30am time: it's the same 8:30 open from Lesson 36, which is not a coincidence.

## What news days do to the day

Three patterns repeat:

1. **The day before is dead.** The market consolidates ahead of a big release, building the liquidity the release will run. Lesson 27 told you what to do in consolidation: nothing.
2. **The release itself is two-stage.** The first move after the print is often the manipulation, a sweep of one side. The real delivery comes after, sometimes minutes later, sometimes not until 10am.
3. **The rest of the day, and the week, is direction.** A big print often sets the bias for several sessions. The day after CPI or FOMC is frequently one of the cleanest trend days of the month.

## Two-stage delivery

This is Power of Three compressed into a few minutes:

> **On a release, expect a fast move that runs liquidity on one side, then a reversal that delivers to the other. The first move is usually the wrong one.**

So the rule for the print itself is the Judas swing rule: don't chase the first candle. Let it sweep, watch for the shift, then treat the market normally. Many traders simply won't enter anything in the fifteen minutes around a release, and take the setup that forms in the kill zone afterwards. That's a perfectly good rule.

## FOMC is different

The Fed decision at 2pm and the press conference at 2:30pm produce two shocks half an hour apart, and the second often reverses the first. It's the single hardest afternoon of the month to trade, and it's inside the New York PM kill zone, which makes it tempting. Most people are better off treating FOMC day from 2pm as closed and trading the day after, when the direction is set.

## The rule

Before every session, ask one question: is there a high-impact release inside my kill zone today?

- **No.** Trade the plan from Lesson 36.
- **Yes, before the kill zone.** Wait for the two-stage delivery to finish, then trade the shift.
- **Yes, inside the kill zone.** Stand aside for the release, and be strict about it.
- **FOMC day.** Trade the morning if you must, and close the afternoon.

This is risk management as much as timing. The Module 3 rule that the stop is set by structure assumes the market respects structure. In the first seconds of a release it doesn't, and spreads widen, so a normal stop can fill somewhere it shouldn't.

## Key terms

- **High-impact release**: NFP, CPI, FOMC and the few others that move every market
- **Two-stage delivery**: the false move on the print, then the true one
- **The day before**: usually consolidation; don't trade it
- **The day after**: often the cleanest direction of the month

## Common mistakes

- **Not knowing the calendar.** It takes two minutes a week.
- **Chasing the print.** The first move is the sweep.
- **Trading through FOMC's afternoon.** Two shocks, half an hour apart.
- **Using a normal stop during a release.** Spreads widen. Stand aside instead.
