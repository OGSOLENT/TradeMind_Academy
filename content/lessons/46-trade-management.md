## The question this lesson answers

Module 3 sized the position and set the stop by structure. Module 7 and 10 got you in. This lesson is everything between the entry and the exit, which is where most people give back the edge they earned.

## Decide before you're in

> **Every management decision is made before entry, written down, and not renegotiated while the trade is open.**

That's the only rule that matters. A trade in progress is the worst possible place to think. So the plan below is a default: use it as written until your journal (next lesson) gives you a reason to change it.

## The default plan

| Point | Action |
|---|---|
| Entry | Full position, stop by structure, as in Module 3 |
| +1R (price has moved the distance of your risk) | Take a partial: close a third to a half. Move the stop to break-even. |
| First target (the nearest liquidity) | Close another portion, or all of it if the target was the whole plan |
| Beyond | Trail the remainder behind structure until stopped or the final draw is hit |

Three things that plan gets right without any thinking on the day: it locks a profit before the trade can become a loss, it pays you at the target the model was built for, and it leaves a piece on for the days the move runs.

## Partials

A partial is closing part of the position. It reduces the reward on the piece you closed and it reduces the pain on everything that follows. Whether it's mathematically optimal depends on your win rate, and your journal will tell you. What it's *always* good for is behaviour: a trader who has banked something manages the rest calmly. Do the maths later; do the partial now.

## Break-even

Moving the stop to your entry price after a partial means the worst case for the rest of the position is zero. Two cautions:

- **Don't move to break-even too early.** A stop at entry inside the retracement that the model expects is a stop that gets hit. Wait for +1R, or for the first target, before moving it.
- **Break-even isn't free.** The stop can fill a few ticks worse than entry on a fast move. Treat it as "almost zero", not zero.

## Trailing behind structure

Trailing means moving the stop up (for a long) as price makes progress, so the trade can't give back much. The wrong way is a fixed distance: "trail 10 points". That has nothing to do with the chart and gets hit by noise. The right way uses the structure from Module 5:

> **Trail the stop below the most recent protected swing low. Move it only when a new protected low forms. Never move it back.**

For a long, each new higher low that's been confirmed by a break of the prior high is a new place for the stop. Price can wobble as much as it likes above it. If it breaks the protected low, the trend has changed by your own definition, and you want to be out anyway.

## Targets

The first target is the nearest liquidity in the bias direction, the same one you used to justify the entry. The final target is the day's draw, or the fractal target from Lesson 18. Between them, take partials at the arrays in the way, the ones from the premium stack for a long, because those are where price tends to pause. A target is a place to pay yourself, not a promise the market made.

## The four ways management ruins a model

1. **Widening the stop.** The single fastest way to turn a small loss into a large one.
2. **Cutting winners at the first wobble.** The retracement is expected. The stop is there for a reason.
3. **Adding to a loser.** Averaging down is a second trade with no setup.
4. **Changing the plan mid-trade.** Everything above was decided in calm. The trade is not calm.

## Key terms

- **Partial**: closing part of the position
- **Break-even**: moving the stop to the entry price
- **Trailing**: moving the stop behind new protected swings
- **+1R**: price has moved one unit of your risk in your favour

## Common mistakes

- **Moving to break-even before the retracement.** It gets hit.
- **Trailing by a fixed distance.** Trail by structure.
- **Managing on feel.** The plan was written before entry for a reason.
- **Widening the stop.** Never.
