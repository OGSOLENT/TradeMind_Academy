## The question this lesson answers

Kill zones are hours long. Inside them, the actual moves cluster into much shorter windows. This lesson gives you those windows, so you're paying attention at the right twenty minutes instead of the whole three hours.

## What a macro is

> **Macro.** A roughly 20-minute window, at a fixed clock time, in which the algorithm is most likely to run liquidity, displace, and rebalance a gap.

The word comes from programming. A macro is a short routine that runs on a schedule. That's the claim here: at certain minutes the delivery algorithm runs a routine, and the routine looks like a sweep, then a displacement, then a return to the gap it left.

You don't need to believe the mechanism to use the observation. The observation is that the biggest intraday moves cluster around a few repeating times.

## The New York macros

| Macro | New York time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-open | 8:50 to 9:10 | Around the 8:30 data and the pre-market |
| **AM 1** | **9:50 to 10:10** | The most watched. It opens the Silver Bullet hour. |
| AM 2 | 10:50 to 11:10 | Closes the first 90 minutes of floor trading |
| Lunch | 11:50 to 12:10 | Often a reversal or the start of the midday range |
| PM 1 | 1:10 to 1:40 | The afternoon session getting going |
| PM 2 | 2:50 to 3:10 | The last real macro before the close |
| Close | 3:15 to 3:45 | The final expansion or the unwind |

London has its own set, most commonly 2:33 to 3:00 and 4:03 to 4:30. The New York times are the ones most traders keep.

## The 90-minute cycle

The macros aren't scattered at random. From the 9:30 equity open, the day tends to organise itself into 90-minute blocks: 9:30 to 11:00, 11:00 to 12:30, and so on. Each block tends to hold one expansion and one retracement, and the macros sit at the boundaries and midpoints of those blocks. That's why 10:50 to 11:10 matters: it's the end of the first block, where the morning's move is often completed or reversed.

## What happens inside a macro

The routine, in order:

1. **The run.** Price reaches for the nearest pool of liquidity, an old high or low, often the one built in the quiet minutes before.
2. **The displacement.** A fast move away from that pool, leaving a fair value gap.
3. **The rebalance.** A return to the gap, and then continuation toward the next draw.

Sometimes only the first two happen inside the twenty minutes and the rebalance comes after. But if none of the three happens, the macro was quiet and the next one is the one to watch.

## Trading a macro

You don't enter because the clock says 9:50. You enter because the routine has played out and you have a setup:

1. Before the macro, know your bias and the nearest liquidity on each side.
2. As the macro opens, watch for the run against your bias. That's the sweep.
3. Look for the displacement back in the bias direction and the gap it leaves.
4. Enter in the gap, in the same way as every other entry in this course.

The macro just tells you when to be looking. It's a timer for your attention.

## Where the Silver Bullet fits

The 9:50 to 10:10 macro is the opening of the 10am to 11am hour, and that hour is the Silver Bullet window, next lesson. The macro is often where the Silver Bullet's gap gets made.

## Key terms

- **Macro**: a scheduled 20-minute window of fast delivery
- **9:50 to 10:10**: the most-watched New York AM macro
- **90-minute cycle**: the blocks the day organises itself into from 9:30
- **The routine**: run, displace, rebalance

## Common mistakes

- **Entering on the clock.** The macro is when to look, not when to click.
- **Watching only 9:50.** The 10:50 and 2:50 macros are often cleaner.
- **Expecting every macro to move.** Some are quiet. Wait for the next.
- **Forgetting the sweep.** The routine starts with a run of liquidity.
