## The trader's dilemma

You have a bias (Lessons 12–13). Price is moving against it. Is this the shallow opposing run you were told to expect — or is your bias simply wrong?

That is the daily wick problem, and it is where most of a trading day's mistakes are made.

## The daily wick

> **Daily wick — the initial price run after the open, before the market expands in its true intended direction.**

It is Lesson 4's opposing run at daily scale, and it does a job: it grabs the liquidity that fuels the real move. The wick is the manipulation phase of AMD (Lesson 7) drawn on the daily chart.

Two rules:

- **Do not arbitrarily guess the bottom.** Guessing where the wick ends is the single most expensive habit in this method.
- **Wait for mechanical proof.** You need a locked-in low, not a hopeful one.

The sequence: **manipulative open forms the wick → mechanical proof appears → expansion in the true direction.**

## The session volatility matrix

Different sessions behave differently, so the timeframe you confirm on should change with the session:

| Session | Volatility | CISD timeframe | Closure timeframe |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Asia** | Low | 1-Hour | 4-Hour |
| **London** | Medium | 15 / 30-Minute | 4-Hour |
| **New York** | High | 5 / 15-Minute | 1-Hour |

Read it as a resolution dial. In quiet Asian hours a 5-minute CISD is noise, so you confirm on the hourly. In the high-volatility New York session the 5-minute carries real information and waiting for an hourly close would mean missing the move entirely.

Matching the confirmation timeframe to the session's volatility is what stops you being both too slow and too twitchy.

## The confirmation workflow

Three steps:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1. Confirm the wick** | Use the session matrix to pick the right timeframe and confirm the wick is complete |
| **2. Lower-timeframe model** | Drop to a lower-timeframe model — a 15-minute entry |
| **3. Set target** | Target the Daily Open, or a 2R return |

Note the two target options. The **daily open** is a structural target — price returning to where the day began. **2R** is a mathematical one — twice what you risked. Either is defensible; drifting between them mid-trade is not.

## The New York strategy

Two specific rules for the highest-volatility session:

- **Avoid the 5-minute pre-open.** Before the bell, the 5-minute chart produces signals that are pure noise.
- **Use 5-minute / 3-minute post-open.** Once the session is running, those timeframes carry information.

## Why bias always wins

Here is the honest part, and the reason this lesson sits where it does:

> **You can mechanically confirm the wick... and still be wrong.**

Confirmation is not certainty. A perfectly valid CISD inside a perfectly formed wick can still fail, because the higher-timeframe bias was wrong to begin with. When bias and confirmation disagree, **bias wins** — a well-executed entry against the daily draw is still a trade in the wrong direction.

This is why Module 5 comes before the fractal model: no amount of execution precision rescues a wrong directional read.

## Complex days and SMT

Some days do not cooperate. The New York open breaks the clean structure, and confirmation on one asset is ambiguous.

The tool for these days is **SMT Divergence** — comparing correlated assets:

> **SMT Divergence — when correlated assets fail to confirm each other's highs and lows, signalling reversal.**

If the Nasdaq makes a lower low but the S&P 500 does not, that "correlation crack" is evidence one of them is being manipulated and a reversal is near. Full treatment is Lesson 20.

## Letting the wick form, or becoming part of it?

The closing question. Traders who guess the bottom of the daily wick *become* the liquidity that completes it. Traders who wait for the session-appropriate confirmation trade the expansion that follows.

## Key terms

- **Daily wick** — the initial run after the open, before the true expansion
- **Session matrix** — session volatility mapped to confirmation timeframes
- **2R** — a target of twice the amount risked
- **Daily open** — the day's opening price, used as a structural target
- **SMT divergence** — correlated assets failing to confirm each other

## Common mistakes

- **Guessing the bottom of the wick.** Named explicitly in the lesson as the thing not to do.
- **Using one confirmation timeframe for every session.** Asia and New York need different resolutions.
- **Assuming confirmation overrides bias.** It does not.
