## The contract for difference

> **A CFD is a private contract with your broker to exchange the difference between an instrument's price when you open the position and its price when you close it.**

Read that against the futures definition from Lesson 55 and the differences jump out. Nothing is standardised by an exchange; the broker writes the contract. Nothing is centrally cleared; the broker is your counterparty, and the price you trade is the price the broker quotes, usually derived from the real market with a spread added. Nothing expires; the position rolls day to day, and you pay a financing charge for every night you hold it.

That's not a criticism. It's the design, and it has real advantages: you can trade a US index in tiny size with no exchange fees, from a phone, at hours the exchange is shut. But you should know what you're holding.

## Spread betting

In the UK and Ireland there's a second form of the same idea.

> **A spread bet is a wager of a fixed amount per point on the direction of a price. Win a point, win your stake per point; lose a point, lose it.**

Mechanically it's a CFD with the size expressed as "£ per point" instead of contracts, and for the trader on the screen it feels identical. Legally and for tax it isn't:

| | CFD | Spread bet (UK) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal form | A derivative contract | A bet |
| Profits | Within Capital Gains Tax | Free of CGT and stamp duty for an individual (HMRC treats it as gambling) |
| Losses | Can be set against capital gains | Cannot be set against anything |
| Availability | Worldwide (not to US residents) | UK and Ireland only |

The tax point is why spread betting exists. Two cautions. HMRC can still tax spread betting where it's part of a trade you already carry on, and the tax-free status is a matter of current HMRC practice, not a law you can rely on forever. If you make a living from it, ask a qualified adviser.

## The retail protections

Since 2018 in Europe (ESMA) and 2019 in the UK (FCA), and since 2021 in Australia (ASIC), retail CFD accounts come with a set of rules that don't exist on a futures account.

**Leverage caps**, by asset:

| Asset | Maximum retail leverage |
|---|---|
| Major currency pairs | 30:1 |
| Non-major pairs, gold, major indices | 20:1 |
| Other commodities, minor indices | 10:1 |
| Individual shares | 5:1 |
| Crypto | 2:1 |

**Margin close-out.** The broker must close your positions when your account equity falls to 50% of the margin required. You can't be left with a runaway position.

**Negative balance protection.** You can't lose more than the money in the account. On a futures account, you can.

**The loss disclosure.** Every CFD broker must state the share of its retail accounts that lose money. The numbers are usually between 65 and 80 per cent, and they're on the front page of the site because the regulator put them there. Read it as data, not marketing.

**Professional status** removes all of this: higher leverage, no close-out rule, no negative balance protection. Brokers will offer to reclassify you. Unless you're already trading size for a living, say no.

In the **US**, CFDs aren't available to retail clients at all; the equivalent products are futures and, for currencies, retail forex with a 50:1 cap.

## Where the costs are

Futures costs are line items. CFD costs are baked in, and you need to know where.

- **The spread.** The broker quotes a buy and a sell price around the market. The gap is the first cost of every trade, paid on entry. On a major index in the cash session it's small; overnight and around news it widens, sometimes a lot.
- **Overnight financing.** Hold a position past the broker's daily cut-off and you pay (or occasionally receive) an interest charge on the full notional value. Over weeks it adds up, and it's why CFDs suit day trading better than holding.
- **Commission.** Some brokers charge it on shares; most build everything into the spread on indices.
- **Slippage and requotes.** Because the broker makes the price, your fill depends on the broker's execution, not on a central book. This is the thing to test in a demo before you trust a broker with money.

## Index CFDs in practice

The instruments you'll see are named for the index, not the future: "US500" or "SPX500" for the S&P, "US100" or "NAS100" for the Nasdaq, "UK100" for the FTSE. They're priced per point, often with a mini or micro size that's a fraction of a pound or dollar per point, so a beginner can risk pennies. The chart is the same chart you've learned to read; the broker's price will track the future closely in the cash session and drift a little outside it.

## Regulation and protection

An FCA-authorised broker holds your money in segregated client accounts, and if it fails, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme covers eligible claims up to £85,000. EU brokers have national equivalents under MiFID II. An offshore broker offering 500:1 leverage has none of this, and the leverage is the reason it's offshore.

## Key terms

- **CFD**: a contract with your broker to exchange the price difference
- **Spread bet**: a stake per point on a price move; tax-free for UK individuals under current practice
- **Leverage cap**: the retail maximum, 30:1 down to 2:1 by asset
- **Negative balance protection**: you can't owe the broker more than your balance
- **Overnight financing**: the daily interest charge on a held CFD
- **Loss disclosure**: the published share of losing retail accounts

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the broker's price as the market.** It's derived from the market, and it's the broker's.
- **Holding CFDs for weeks.** The financing charge is designed to make that expensive.
- **Taking professional status for the leverage.** You give up every protection to get it.
- **Ignoring the loss disclosure.** It's the most honest number on the site.
