## The question this lesson answers

Lesson 11 taught you the order block, the last opposing candle before a displacement. This lesson answers what happens when an order block fails. The answer, surprisingly, is that it gets more useful.

## The failure that flips

An order block is meant to hold. Price returns to it, finds the orders that started the last move, and continues. Sometimes it doesn't. Price trades straight through the block with displacement. Most people delete the level at that point. Don't.

> **Breaker block.** A failed order block that flips to act from the opposite side, after a liquidity sweep.

The sweep is the detail that matters. Here's the full sequence for a bearish breaker:

1. Price makes a swing high.
2. Price pulls back, forming a bullish order block (a down candle) on the way.
3. Price rallies above the old swing high, sweeping the buy stops above it.
4. Price reverses and displaces down through the bullish order block.
5. That bullish block is now a **bearish breaker**. Price returning up into it is expected to sell off.

The bullish breaker is the mirror: a swing low, a bearish order block on the bounce, a sweep of the sell stops below the low, then a displacement up through the block. The old bearish block now acts as support.

## Why the sweep matters

Without the sweep, you just have a level that broke, and levels break all day. The sweep is the tell that something real happened: the stops beyond the swing were taken, which means the move that followed had fuel behind it. The people who bought the breakout above the old high are now trapped. When price comes back up to the breaker, they're selling to get out, and that's your reaction.

A failed block *without* a sweep is a different array with a different job. That's the mitigation block, next lesson.

## Reading the breaker

For a bearish breaker, the zone you mark is the body of the failed bullish order block, the down candle. Some traders use the whole candle including wicks. The body is the tighter, more precise version and it's the one to start with.

| Breaker | Formed from | Acts as | Enter on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bearish | A bullish order block that failed after a sweep of a high | Resistance | Price returning up into it |
| Bullish | A bearish order block that failed after a sweep of a low | Support | Price returning down into it |

## The entry and the stop

Price returns to the breaker, usually as a retracement into the new expansion. You want:

- The return to happen inside a kill zone.
- A lower-timeframe confirmation as it touches: a CISD or a small structure shift, from Module 5.
- The stop just beyond the far side of the breaker. If price closes through a breaker, the breaker has failed twice and you don't want to be there.

That stop is why breakers are prized. The zone is small, so the risk is small, and the target is the liquidity on the other side of the range.

## Breakers and gaps

The most reliable breakers have a fair value gap sitting inside them or right beside them, because the displacement that flipped the block is the same move that left the gap. When the two overlap you've got the unicorn, which Lesson 32 covers. When you're choosing between two breakers, take the one with the gap.

## Key terms

- **Breaker block**: a failed order block, flipped by a displacement after a sweep
- **Bearish breaker**: a failed bullish block that now acts as resistance
- **Bullish breaker**: a failed bearish block that now acts as support
- **The sweep**: the run of stops that has to come before the flip

## Common mistakes

- **Deleting a broken order block.** It just changed jobs.
- **Calling any broken level a breaker.** No sweep, no breaker.
- **Marking the wicks and getting a fat zone.** Start with the body.
- **Staying in after a close through the breaker.** That's a second failure. Get out.
