## The question this lesson answers

Two of the most precise entries in the method come from stacking things you already know. This lesson builds them.

## The inversion gap entry

Lesson 7a told you an invalidated fair value gap flips its function. A bullish gap that gets closed through becomes resistance. Here's that idea as a trade.

> **Inversion FVG entry.** After a gap is closed through with displacement, wait for price to return to it from the other side and enter on the rejection.

For a short:

1. A bullish fair value gap sits below price. It's been acting as support.
2. Price displaces down and closes through it. The gap is invalidated and is now an inversion gap, bearish.
3. Price retraces back up into the inverted gap.
4. Enter short in the gap. Stop above the gap's high. Target the liquidity below.

The bullish version is the mirror. The entry works because the gap has changed hands. The buyers who were defending it have been run over, and the return to it is where they exit and where new sellers press.

### The confirmation

Don't enter on the touch alone. Look for the lower-timeframe CISD from Lesson 7, or a small structure shift, as price reaches the inverted gap. The gap tells you where. The confirmation tells you when.

## The unicorn

The name is a joke about how rarely the alignment happens, and the alignment is this:

> **Unicorn.** A breaker block and a fair value gap overlapping at the same price.

Recall how a breaker forms: sweep, then displacement through an old block. That displacement is fast, so it very often leaves a gap behind. Sometimes that gap sits exactly on the failed block. When it does, you've got two independent reasons for price to react at one zone.

| Array | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| Breaker | Trapped traders, the reason for the reversal |
| Fair value gap | The imbalance, the reason price gets drawn back |

Together they give you a zone that's small, well-reasoned, and easy to define the risk on.

## Trading the unicorn

1. Find the breaker as in Lesson 30: a sweep, then a displacement through an old block.
2. Check whether the displacement left a gap that overlaps the breaker's body. If not, it's just a breaker. Fine, but not a unicorn.
3. Mark the overlap, the zone where both arrays sit. Mark its 50%, the consequent encroachment.
4. Wait for price to return to the overlap inside a kill zone.
5. Enter on the lower-timeframe confirmation. Stop beyond the far side of the overlap. Target the opposing liquidity.

Because the overlap is usually smaller than either array on its own, the stop is tight and the reward to risk is strong. That's the whole appeal.

## Stacking, in general

Both of these are examples of one principle: one array is a level, two arrays at the same price are a setup. When you have to choose between zones, prefer the one where more of the matrix agrees. A rejection block on its own is weak. A rejection block sitting on an order block that has a gap inside it is a different thing altogether.

## Key terms

- **Inversion gap entry**: entering on the return to an invalidated, flipped gap
- **Unicorn**: a breaker block overlapping a fair value gap
- **Overlap**: the shared price span of two arrays
- **Consequent encroachment**: the 50% of the overlap, or of any gap

## Common mistakes

- **Entering an inverted gap on the touch.** Wait for the confirmation.
- **Calling a breaker with a nearby gap a unicorn.** The gap has to overlap the block.
- **Marking the whole breaker instead of the overlap.** The overlap is the zone.
- **Forgetting time.** A perfect unicorn at the wrong hour is still a level, not a trade.
