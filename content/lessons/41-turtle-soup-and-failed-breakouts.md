## The question this lesson answers

The 2022 model waits for a sweep and then a shift. Turtle soup is what you do when the sweep *is* the setup, and you want to be in before the shift has fully formed. It's faster, it's tighter, and it's less forgiving.

## The name

In the 1980s a group of traders called the Turtles were taught to buy breakouts above the 20-day high and sell breakouts below the 20-day low. It worked, for a while, and then it became so well known that the breakouts started failing. Linda Raschke and Larry Connors published a setup that faded those failed breakouts, and named it turtle soup, because it cooked the Turtles in their own rules.

The original had precise mechanics: a 20-day low, a break below it, then a buy stop back above the old low. ICT kept the idea and dropped the fixed lookback. In this method, any clean level with stops beyond it can be the turtle's channel.

## The ICT version

> **Turtle soup.** Price sweeps a marked high or low, fails to hold beyond it, and trades back inside. You enter on the failure, against the breakout.

The marked levels are the ones you've been using all along: the previous day's high and low, session highs and lows, equal highs and lows, the Asian range. Anywhere stops are stacked.

A bullish turtle soup:

1. Price is above a clean, obvious low with stops beneath it.
2. Price trades through the low. The breakout traders sell, the stops trigger.
3. Within a candle or two, price closes back *above* the low. The breakout has failed.
4. Enter long on that close, or on the next small pullback toward the low.
5. Stop just below the sweep's wick. Target the liquidity above.

## Why the stop can be so close

The whole logic of the trade is that the low was swept and rejected. If price trades back below the sweep's wick, the rejection was false and the trade is simply wrong. So the stop doesn't need room: it goes a few ticks under the wick. That's why turtle soup trades often have three or four to one on the risk. It's also why they fail more often than the 2022 model: you're in before the shift has confirmed.

## Turtle soup versus the 2022 model

| | Turtle soup | 2022 model |
|---|---|---|
| Enters | On the failed breakout, before the shift | On the gap, after the shift |
| Stop | Just beyond the sweep wick | Beyond the sweep low, below the gap |
| Reward to risk | Higher | Lower, but more reliable |
| Win rate | Lower | Higher |
| Best for | Clean, obvious levels in a kill zone with a strong bias | Everything else |

They're the same event, the sweep, traded at two different moments. Turtle soup is the aggressive version. If you're new, run the 2022 model first. Once you've seen a few hundred sweeps, turtle soup will look obvious, and you'll know which levels deserve it.

## The tells that improve it

- **Bias agreement.** A bullish turtle soup on a bullish day is a retracement trade. On a bearish day it's a counter-trend scalp with a short life.
- **A kill zone.** Sweeps outside the windows fail more, and the ones that work tend to drift rather than displace.
- **The candle.** A long wick that closes back inside the range in one candle is the classic. A slow grind back inside is weaker.
- **Equal levels.** Equal lows with stops under them are the best turtle soup lows there are.

## Key terms

- **Turtle soup**: fading a failed breakout of a marked level
- **The Turtles**: the breakout traders the setup was named against
- **Failed breakout**: a sweep that closes back inside the range
- **Sweep wick**: where the stop sits

## Common mistakes

- **Fading every breakout.** Only marked levels with stops beyond them, in a window.
- **Giving the stop room.** Beyond the wick is the whole idea.
- **Running it against a strong bias.** It becomes a scalp with a short shelf life.
- **Starting here.** Learn the 2022 model first; this is the aggressive version of it.
